Talking to your data: why natural language analytics needs a semantic layer

We built a tool that lets anyone on the team ask questions of company data in plain English. It only worked once we built a semantic layer that teaches the AI what our terms mean and how our datasets connect.

Summary

Natural language analytics lets anyone ask a question like “How are bookings trending week over week?” and get an instant answer, chart or forecast. It works easily on one simple dataset. It breaks when you connect datasets, because systems name the same thing differently (account_id in the CRM, sold_to_party in finance) and teams define metrics like revenue differently. No off-the-shelf tool can fix that, because the knowledge is implicit and lives in people’s heads. The fix is a semantic layer: a written definition of your terms, how datasets relate, and your business rules, which then becomes a composable knowledge layer any AI tool can use.

Key points

  • Asking data questions in plain English works well on one simple dataset.
  • It breaks when you join datasets: the same customer is account_id in the CRM and sold_to_party in finance.
  • Teams also define the same metric differently, like revenue or “active customer.”
  • Vendors can’t fix this automatically, because the knowledge is implicit, not written down.
  • A semantic layer makes it explicit: what terms mean, how datasets relate, and how the business calculates things.
  • Building it takes real effort from the people who hold the context, but the result works with any AI tool.

What natural language analytics can do

We just built a tool that lets us talk to our data. Anyone on our team can ask a question like “Which geographies tend to convert the most leads?” or “How are bookings trending week over week?” And it’ll instantly generate the answer for them. It can make charts and visuals on the fly, and we can even use it to start making predictions based on our historical data.

One dataset is easy. Connecting many is hard.

So, when ChatGPT first came out, this was one of those use cases that everybody immediately jumped to. And it actually worked pretty well, as long as you were working with one relatively simple dataset. The problem comes when you need to start connecting different datasets, which is what pretty much every company needs to do.

That’s because, for instance, in your CRM a customer might be referred to by account_id, and in your finance system that same customer might be called the sold_to_party.

It gets even messier when you realize that marketing and finance might have completely different definitions of something like revenue, or what counts as an active customer.

Why off-the-shelf tools can’t fix it

The reason a vendor or off-the-shelf tool can’t just fix this automatically is that your organization has spent years accumulating knowledge about how to interpret its own data. That’s implicit rather than explicit context, and no tool can understand it unless you teach it how to understand it.

The fix: a semantic layer

So we discovered that what we actually needed is something called a semantic layer. Basically, it’s a way of teaching the AI what all of our terms mean, how the different datasets relate to each other, and what rules our business uses to calculate things.

More work than we expected, and worth it

That’s why building natural language analytics took a lot more effort than we first thought it would. We needed the people who keep all of this implicit context in their heads to sit down and define all of it for the machines. The good news is, now that we’ve done it, we have a composable knowledge layer that any AI tool can tap into.
